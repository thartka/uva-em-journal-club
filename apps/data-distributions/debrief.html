If the data are… Describe the patients with And compare groups with
Approximately normal
symmetric, single peak, mean − 2 SD is a possible value		 Mean ± SD.
Add a 95% CI if you want the precision of the mean.		 t-test, ANOVA, linear regression
Skewed
long tail one way; mean and median disagree		 Median with IQR, or the range. Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis
Bimodal, plateau, or otherwise odd Show the figure. Report the subgroups separately if they are clinically distinct. Non-parametric, but think hard about whether one comparison even answers the question
  • The shape decides the summary and the test. This is the one decision that gets made before any analysis and is almost never reported.
  • Mean ± SD is only for approximately normal data. The fastest check needs no software: if mean − 2 SD is impossible for the variable (a negative age, a negative length of stay), the distribution is skewed and the mean ± SD you are reading is misleading.
  • Mean ≈ median is evidence of symmetry, not of normality. A bimodal distribution is perfectly symmetric about a centre where hardly any patients sit. Look at the figure.
  • SD and CI answer different questions. The SD describes the spread of the patients; a confidence interval describes the precision of the estimate. A narrow CI never rescues a skewed variable.
  • "Always non-parametric" is not the lesson. On genuinely normal data the parametric test is the more powerful one. Match the method to the shape. That is the whole skill.

Reading a paper this week? Go to Table 1 and check every continuous variable for a SD larger than about half the mean on something that cannot be negative. Length of stay, time to antibiotics, door-to-balloon, troponin. That is where you will find it.
