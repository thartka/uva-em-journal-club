P-values are a challenging concept, but when correctly interpreted, can be a powerful statistical device.

A p-value IS… A p-value is NOT…
P(data this extreme | H₀ true) — how surprising the data are if there's no real effect. the probability that H₀ is true, or that the treatment works (Q1, Q2).
a measure that shrinks with a larger sample (less variability). a measure of effect size, clinical importance, or “strength of evidence” (Q3).
one data point to interpret alongside the effect size and its confidence interval. proof of “no effect” when it's above 0.05 — that may just be an imprecise study (Q4).
  • Q1 — Definition: the p-value is the tail area under the null. It answers “how surprising are these data if nothing is going on?” — not “is the null true?”
  • Q2 — Direction of the conditional: P(data | H₀) is not P(H₀ | data). When most coins are fair, a surprising flip usually still came from a fair coin. A small p-value is not the chance the null is true.
  • Q3 — Precision, not magnitude: the same 5 mmHg effect went from p = 0.30 to p < 0.001 on sample size alone. A smaller p means a more precise estimate, not a bigger or more important one.
  • Q4 — Non-significant ≠ no effect: the same 15% risk reduction that was “negative” at 500 patients became significant at ≈1,200. A non-significant result from a small trial means “we don’t know yet” — read the CI, and ask whether a larger trial is warranted.
  • Bottom line: a p-value is one piece of evidence. Always combine it with the effect size, the confidence interval, and clinical judgment.
