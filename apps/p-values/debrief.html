One definition does all the work: a p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. Everything a p-value is not follows from that.

A p-value IS… A p-value is NOT…
P(data this extreme | H₀ true) — how surprising the data are if there's no real effect. the probability that H₀ is true, or that the treatment works (Q1, Q2).
a measure that shrinks with more precision (bigger n, less variability). a measure of effect size, clinical importance, or “strength of evidence” (Q3).
one input to interpret alongside the effect size and its confidence interval. proof of “no effect” when it's above 0.05 — that may just be an imprecise study (Q4).
  • Q1 — Definition: the p-value is the tail area under the null. It answers “how surprising are these data if nothing is going on?” — not “is the null true?”
  • Q2 — Direction of the conditional: P(data | H₀) is not P(H₀ | data). When most coins are fair, a surprising flip usually still came from a fair coin. A small p-value is not the chance the null is true.
  • Q3 — Precision, not magnitude: the same 1 mmHg effect went from p = 0.30 to p < 0.001 on sample size alone. A smaller p means a more precise estimate, not a bigger or more important one.
  • Q4 — Read the CI: two non-significant trials can mean opposite things. A wide CI is inconclusive; a tight CI hugging the null actually rules out a meaningful effect. The confidence interval tells them apart.
  • Bottom line: a p-value is one piece of evidence. Always combine it with the effect size, the confidence interval, and clinical judgment.
